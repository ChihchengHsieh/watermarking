\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

{\bf Diffusion watermarking.}
Watermarking for generative models provides machine-detectable provenance while preserving image quality. Post-hoc methods embed watermarks after image generation, e.g., learned encoder-decoder frameworks such as HiDDeN~\cite{zhu2018hidden}, but are vulnerable to common editing operations. In-generation methods instead embed the watermark during image synthesis. Representative examples include Tree-Ring, which injects Fourier-domain ring patterns into the initial diffusion noise~\cite{wen2023tree_ring}, and Stable Signature, which embeds a recoverable signature by modifying the decoder~\cite{fernandez2023stable}. Other approaches explore latent-space watermarking, distribution-preserving embedding, and robustness to attacks~\cite{zhang2024attack,yang2024gaussian,huang2024robin}. SpiderMark follows the in-generation paradigm, replacing isotropic ring patterns with a spider-web frequency pattern and a learned probabilistic verifier.

{\bf Learned watermark verification.}
Classical frequency-domain detectors often rely on handcrafted correlations, $\ell_1$ distances, PSNR-like scores, or fixed thresholds.
Such detectors are interpretable and lightweight, but their operating thresholds can shift substantially across transformations.
SpiderMark addresses this issue with a learned verifier that processes recovered frequency-domain representations and auxiliary watermark-aligned cues.
The present work keeps this verifier-centric view but asks how the verifier should be trained when robustness depends on the distribution of downstream perturbations.

{\bf Meta-learning and episodic robustness.}
Gradient-based meta-learning methods such as MAML~\cite{finn2017maml} and Reptile~\cite{nichol2018reptile} optimize model parameters so that a small number of task-specific updates yields strong query performance.
ANIL studies whether MAML's gains come from rapid full-network adaptation or from feature reuse, and performs inner-loop updates only on the task-specific head~\cite{raghu2020rapid}.
Metric-based meta-learners such as Matching Networks~\cite{vinyals2016matching} and Prototypical Networks~\cite{snell2017prototypical} classify queries by comparing support and query embeddings, while solver-based meta-learners such as R2D2~\cite{bertinetto2019meta} and MetaOptNet~\cite{lee2019metaoptnet} adapt closed-form or convex classifiers on top of learned embeddings.
This idea is especially relevant when training and testing conditions are organized as related tasks rather than i.i.d. samples.
For watermark verification, each downstream transformation induces a related but distinct classification task: the semantic labels remain clean versus watermarked, but the visible and spectral evidence changes.
Agentic MetaSpiderMark uses this structure directly by sampling attack-conditioned support and query sets, then using their query feedback to guide future task exposure.


{\bf Task scheduling, curricula, and agentic control.}
The meta-learning objective determines how the verifier adapts within each task, but task scheduling also plays a critical role. Uniform and cyclic sampling provide stable coverage, while curriculum and hard-example mining prioritize difficult tasks to improve generalization~\cite{bengio2009curriculum}. Recent adaptive schedulers learn task-selection policies from model-state signals, including ATS~\cite{yao2021ats}, BASS~\cite{ban2023bass}, Adaptive Sampler~\cite{wang2024tasksampler}, GCP~\cite{liu2020adaptive}, and DERTS~\cite{zhan2024derts}. Agentic AI offers a complementary approach: instead of learning a fixed scheduler or relying on scalar rewards, an agent reasons over online training statistics to adaptively update task weights. Agentic MetaSpiderMark follows this paradigm by using verifier feedback to generate constrained task-weight updates for subsequent episodes. Our benchmark compares the proposed agentic scheduler with non-agentic alternatives, isolating the benefits of closed-loop task scheduling from those of verifier meta-learning.
