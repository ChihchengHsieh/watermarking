Before studying verifier meta-learning, we compare candidate watermarking backbones under dataset shift.

SpiderMark obtains the highest accuracy and AUROC under every reported attack while retaining an FID close to the non-watermarked reference.
We therefore keep SpiderMark as the fixed watermarking backbone and study whether its verifier training can be improved through attack-conditioned meta-learning and adaptive task scheduling.

\paragraph{Decision-trace analysis.}
The scheduler trace contains 31 agent updates between global steps 1 and 1902.
Each update stores the signed residual vector, the compact task statistics, and a natural-language rationale.
The run starts with a conservative residual budget of $\pm 0.15$ and switches at meta-training step 400 to a wider budget of $\pm 0.5$.
This makes the early scheduler behavior cautious, while giving the agent enough authority later to suppress attacks that appear saturated or unproductive.
The rationales show that the agent does not behave as a simple hard-task miner.
Instead, it repeatedly weighs four kinds of evidence: attack difficulty from recent query loss, uncertainty from loss volatility, coverage from usage and recency, and usefulness from recent reward or loss improvement.
Across the trace, the rationales frequently cite over-sampling and under-sampling, high or low loss, uncertainty, reward, recency, and balance.
This makes the scheduler auditable: the heatmap gives the action, while the rationale explains why the action was chosen.

The most visible pattern is the \texttt{crop} row.
During the middle of training, the agent often increases \texttt{crop} because its recent loss and uncertainty remain high.
Later, when \texttt{crop} has saturated usage but poor progress, the rationale switches from ``hard and informative'' to ``over-sampled'' and the residual becomes strongly negative.
This sign reversal is important: the agent is not merely increasing all high-loss tasks; after the residual budget is widened, it can also decide that a hard task should receive less sampling when the trace suggests diminishing returns.
The \texttt{msg\_app} task is typically increased in the early and middle phases because the rationales identify persistent loss and uncertainty under compound platform-style processing.
By contrast, \texttt{occlusion} is often reduced early when it has low loss and low uncertainty, but receives positive residuals later to maintain coverage and stabilize under-sampled regions of the task pool.
Tasks such as \texttt{downup50}, \texttt{jpeg}, and \texttt{clean} usually receive smaller corrections because the agent treats them as stable, improving, or useful mainly for coverage.

\subsection{Best Accuracy-First Performance on Unseen COCO}

We compare the proposed LLM-residual scheduler against uniform sampling, non-contextual UCB bandits~\cite{auer2002finite}, ATS-style adaptive scheduling~\cite{yao2021ats}, BASS-style contextual bandits~\cite{ban2023bass}, and Adaptive Sampler-style task sampling~\cite{wang2024tasksampler}.
All six rows use the same SpiderMark verifier architecture, optimizer, first-order meta-learning update, seven-task attack pool, seed 19,980,802, and 2,000 meta-training steps.
Each episode contains $n_s=8$ support and $n_q=8$ query examples.
Downstream verifier training reuses one shared on-the-fly batch stream across methods, and each method is selected by augmented-validation accuracy with AUROC used only to break accuracy ties.

\paragraph{Test protocol.}
The final test uses all 1,000 images in the held-out COCO verifier dataset, which is not used for meta-training or downstream training.
Every checkpoint sees the same ordered examples under clean, strong JPEG, message-app compound processing, down-up resampling, blur, random crop, occlusion, geometric warp, and mixed training-time augmentation conditions.
Accuracy is measured at the verifier's fixed 0.5 probability threshold.
The primary aggregate is the unweighted mean across the eight non-clean attacks; clean performance is reported separately.
We compute 95\% confidence intervals by resampling image indices jointly across methods and attacks for 2,000 paired bootstrap replicates.
Thus, the intervals quantify test-image uncertainty for this controlled training run, not variation across independent training seeds.
We treat fixed-threshold accuracy as the primary metric because a deployed watermark verifier must convert its score into a binary provenance decision; AUROC remains a secondary measure of threshold-free ranking quality.

\paragraph{Accuracy-first result.}
MetaSpiderMark is the top-ranked scheduler on the primary metric, achieving the highest mean attacked-condition accuracy, 0.8915 (95\% CI: 0.8806--0.9021), followed by BASS at 0.8885 and ATS at 0.8884; no competing method attains a higher mean accuracy.
Its largest condition-level strengths are message-app compound processing and geometric warp, where it records the best accuracy among the six methods.
Because all predictions are paired by test image, we also bootstrap the mean accuracy difference directly.
MetaSpiderMark exceeds uniform sampling by 0.74 percentage points (95\% CI: 0.08--1.41), ASR by 0.99 points (0.29--1.73), and Bandit-UCB by 0.99 points (0.28--1.78).
The smaller margins over BASS, 0.30 points ($-0.41$--1.03), and ATS, 0.31 points ($-0.31$--0.93), are not statistically resolved.
Uniform obtains the highest mean AUROC, 0.9600, compared with 0.9586 for MetaSpiderMark.
Accordingly, the controlled test supports a strong but metric-specific conclusion: Agentic MetaSpiderMark is the best accuracy-first scheduler among the six evaluated methods, significantly outperforming three of five competitors in paired analysis, while BASS and ATS remain statistically competitive and Uniform remains strongest under the threshold-free AUROC metric.

\subsection{Reported Metrics and Remaining Uncertainty}

We report fixed-threshold accuracy as the primary metric and AUROC as the secondary metric because deployed watermark verifiers must make binary decisions at one operating threshold.
The paired bootstrap captures uncertainty from the finite COCO test images while retaining the matched comparison across methods and attacks.
It does not capture meta-training or downstream-training seed variability; additional training seeds remain necessary before attributing small differences among the leading methods to scheduler choice alone.
FID and CLIP image-text similarity from the SpiderMark backbone study remain applicable because the watermark injection mechanism is unchanged, but they are supporting evidence rather than the scheduler comparison axis.

\subsection{Experiment Roadmap}

The controlled single-seed scheduler benchmark is now complete, but further evidence is required before small scheduler differences can be treated as stable.
We organize the remaining experiments around training-seed replication, SOTA/canonical meta-learning context, and cost/quality ablations.
The repository-level execution plan is maintained in \texttt{BENCHMARK\_PLAN.md}; here we summarize the benchmark structure expected in the final paper.

\paragraph{Stage 1: anchor baselines.}
This stage establishes the fixed reference points: the improved non-meta SpiderMark verifier and the currently available meta-trained verifier checkpoints.
It answers whether the direction is worth expanding and provides the non-meta anchor for all later scheduler deltas.

\paragraph{Stage 2: scheduler benchmark.}
This stage is completed for the six-method, single-seed comparison in Table~\ref{tab:controlled_coco_accuracy_first}.
The rows use matched 2,000-step meta-training, shared downstream batches, accuracy-selected checkpoints, and a full paired COCO test.

\paragraph{Stage 3: SOTA/canonical meta-learning comparison.}
After the scheduler comparison is stable, this stage fixes the scheduler and compares FOMAML, MAML, ANIL, Reptile, Matching Networks-style attention, Prototypical Networks-style prototypes, and an R2D2-style differentiable ridge solver head under the same attack pool, number of meta-training steps, support/query sizes, and checkpoint-selection rule.
This separates improvements from the meta-learning update itself from improvements caused by agentic task scheduling.

\paragraph{Stage 4: stability and ablations.}
This stage checks that the scheduler ranking is stable rather than a single-run artifact.
It should include seed variance, checkpoint selection, held-out attacks or held-out prompts, scheduler overhead, and quality metrics for the final selected configuration.
External watermarking baselines can be included as contextual comparisons, but they are not the main benchmark required to support the paper's claim.

The concrete missing items are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item repeat the meta versus no-meta comparison across multiple seeds;
    \item repeat the controlled scheduler benchmark across additional meta-training and downstream-training seeds;
    \item optionally evaluate the GCP-style proxy as an appendix scheduler row;
    \item evaluate FOMAML, MAML, ANIL, Reptile, Matching Networks-style, Prototypical Networks-style, and R2D2-style ridge baselines under the selected fixed scheduler and compute budget;
    \item compare checkpoint-selection strategies beyond the declared accuracy-first rule;
    \item test a second unseen prompt or image distribution beyond the completed COCO transfer evaluation;
    \item measure agent-call overhead, scheduler overhead, image quality, and semantic preservation for the final selected method.
\end{itemize}
